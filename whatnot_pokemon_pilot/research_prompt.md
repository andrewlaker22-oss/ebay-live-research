# Research Phase 1: How Whatnot took off (2019–2022)

You are running a source-driven historical investigation. Accuracy beats coverage. If you cannot find or verify something, say so plainly; do not fill the gap with a plausible guess.

## The question this research must answer

**Did Whatnot succeed because it discovered a new consumer need, or because it found a better way to sell something collecting communities were already doing?**

Every section of your report should help answer that question. If a finding does not bear on it, leave it out or put it in the gaps section.

## Why this matters

The research is for Andy, who works on eBay Live (eBay's livestream-shopping product). eBay is pushing Live from the top down: an ad campaign, homepage placement, notifications and promoted ads leading to Live pages. Whatnot appears to have grown from the bottom up, by turning behaviour that already existed in collector communities into a business. Understanding exactly how Whatnot succeeded tells eBay whether it should be capturing existing community behaviour, creating new behaviour, or both.

Strategy feedback at eBay adds three points to keep in mind:
1. People may not go looking for livestream shopping; it may need to reach them in feeds they already use.
2. Only a small share of people shop live at scale, so the bigger opportunity may be growing the whole category.
3. eBay Live currently measures success as viewers who watch 60 seconds or more.

## What to investigate

**A. What already existed before Whatnot.**
Were collectors already selling, auctioning, breaking or opening products on livestreams (Instagram Live, YouTube, Facebook, Twitch) before Whatnot's live auctions? Who did it, in which categories, and what were the problems (payment, trust, shipping, bidding by comment)? A May 2020 Sports Illustrated article reportedly documented creators livestreaming sports-card openings; TechCrunch's December 2020 coverage reportedly described livestream auctions as already established. Verify both.

**B. What Whatnot did, and when.**
Build the dated sequence: founding, the switch to livestream auctions (reportedly July 2020), each category launch, funding rounds, seller recruitment (Whatnot reportedly recruited sellers with established credibility in each community), marketing and advertising pushes, and product features (bidding, payment, shipping, trust and authentication). TechCrunch reported on Whatnot in December 2020, March 2021, May 2021 and around September 2021.

**C. What happened to demand.**
When did collecting itself boom (sports cards and Pokémon in 2020 to 2021), and what is the dated evidence? How did Whatnot's growth line up with those booms? Start from Sensor Tower's June 2022 blog post (https://sensortower.com/blog/live-stream-shopping-2022) for Whatnot's share of U.S. livestream-shopping app downloads. Say clearly where Whatnot's growth came before, during or after the wider collecting boom.

**D. Which communities drove it.**
Compare Funko, sports cards and Pokémon as growth drivers using whatever sources exist (category share, seller counts, company statements, reporting). Was the largest early category also the earliest? Did collectors move over from somewhere (Instagram Live, eBay, card shops), or did Whatnot bring in people who were new to collecting?

**E. Why buyers came and stayed.**
Retrieve Whatnot's 2024 State of Livestream Selling Report (https://blog.teamwhatnot.com/s/Whatnot_State_of_Livestream_Selling_Report.pdf). Check the July 2024 buyer survey (reported as 3,334 respondents) and its five figures: 66% watched to get a product they wanted, 53% to find deals, 46% for entertainment, 43% to watch a seller they liked, 21% to chat with the community. Say whether it explains why people first joined or only why existing buyers keep watching. Look for any independent (not Whatnot) research on why U.S. consumers shop live. Test this sequence as a hypothesis, not a fact: product interest, then first purchase, then attachment to a seller, then repeat buying.

## The four possible reasons for success (organise the evidence around these)

1. **Existing demand:** people already wanted the products, and live selling was a better way to buy them.
2. **Creators and sellers:** established community sellers brought their audiences, or buyers stayed for particular sellers.
3. **Discovery and marketing:** advertising, app-store growth, referrals and promotion brought people in.
4. **Buying inside the stream:** bidding, payment, shipping and trust built into the live experience fixed what Instagram Live and other platforms lacked.

For each, say whether the evidence supports it, contradicts it, or is too thin to say, and which reason mattered most in which period.

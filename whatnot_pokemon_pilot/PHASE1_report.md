# Phase 1: How Whatnot took off (2019 to 2022)

Researched and written by Claude, 5 October 2026, from public pages read directly (not search summaries) unless marked otherwise. Scope and rules follow `research_prompt.md`.

## 1. The answer first

Whatnot did not discover a new consumer need. It found a better way to sell something collectors were already doing, and it arrived in the middle of a collecting boom it did not cause. Live auctions of collectibles were already running on Instagram Live (Disney pins, Funko) and card breaks were already booming on YouTube and Twitter by spring 2020, months before Whatnot's first livestream in July 2020. What Whatnot added was the plumbing those streams lacked: bidding, payment and shipping inside the stream. The order of what mattered was: **2020, buying inside the stream** (fixing Instagram Live's broken auctions for an existing Funko and pin crowd); **2021, existing demand** (Whatnot plugged into the sports-card and Pokémon boom, and sports cards became its largest category within about four months of launching in January 2021); **2022 onward, paid discovery** (Instagram ad spend up 414% to $3.8M in Jan to May 2022, then "ten times more" marketing in 2025). The creator/seller story is real but thinner in the record than expected: sources show sellers came because they saw results, not a documented campaign to recruit famous community sellers. Confidence: high on the sequence (dated, contemporary sources agree), medium on causation (sources show things coinciding, and company statements, not independent proof of why buyers came).

## 3. The four reasons

### Reason 1: Existing demand (people already wanted the products)

**For.** The products and the buying ritual were both established before Whatnot. Card breaking dates to the mid-2000s. In spring 2020, before Whatnot's first stream, breakers were already selling slots on YouTube and Twitter streams to thousands of viewers (SI, 4 May 2020) and one breaker's May 2020 revenue was $1.1M, up 259% (LA Times). eBay's U.S. trading card sales grew 142% in 2020 and Pokémon 574%. By May 2021 cards were scarce enough that Target pulled them from shelves. Whatnot's own first stream worked because there were already Funko collectors ready to bid.

**Against.** Nothing in the record shows Whatnot customers who were new to collecting in this period. The absence is a gap, not evidence of either side.

**Timing.** Whatnot's growth came during the collecting boom, not before it. Breaks surged from mid-March 2020; Whatnot's first stream was July 2020; it added Pokémon in late 2020 and sports cards in January 2021, roughly ten months after breaking had already spiked. Its largest category (sports cards) was not its earliest (Funko).

**Verdict: supported.** This was the main fuel in 2021.

### Reason 2: Creators and sellers bringing audiences

**For.** Seller growth was visible and seller-led: Funko sellers "heard about the sale and wanted to do their own" (Inc.); LaFontaine: "Any seller who saw that was like, I don't know what that is, but I want it" (NEA, 2023). "Thousands of sellers" by March 2021, "a couple thousand active livestream sellers" by September 2021. Whatnot's Series B release spotlights an existing card business (World Card Traders) that grew to five staff and office space on the app. In 2024, 43% of surveyed Whatnot buyers said they watch "to watch a seller I like."

**Against.** The specific lead that Whatnot *recruited respected sellers with existing credibility in each community* was not found in any source I could read. The founders describe slow, careful onboarding of sellers attracted by results, not a poaching campaign. No source names breakers or Instagram auctioneers who moved over with their audiences in 2020 to 2021.

**Verdict: mixed / too thin to say.** Sellers clearly mattered, but whether they *brought* audiences or *gained* them on Whatnot is not established. This is Phase 2's question.

### Reason 3: Discovery and marketing

**For.** Paid acquisition is clearly part of the scaling story. Ad spend went from $739K (Jan to May 2021) to $3.8M (Jan to May 2022), with 99% on Instagram, and Whatnot's share of top-10 live-shopping-app installs went from 17% to 35% over the same periods. In 2025 Whatnot spent "ten times more in marketing" and new buyers grew 285%.

**Against.** Marketing came after the product was working. Whatnot was growing "at least 100% month-over-month" by December 2020 and sports cards had grown 80x by May 2021 while ad spend was still modest. Installs are not buyers, and rising ad spend and rising installs only coincide in the data; no source isolates the effect.

**Interpretation (mine, not a source's):** spending 99% of ads on Instagram meant advertising where collectors and the original Instagram Live auctions already were. That is closer to capturing an existing audience than creating a new one.

**Verdict: supported for 2022 onward; too thin to say for 2020 to 2021.**

### Reason 4: Buying inside the stream

**For.** This is the one reason contemporary reporting states directly as the gap Whatnot filled. TechCrunch (Dec 2020): Instagram Live "aren't really built for this," with manual payment after the sale and buyers backing out. TechCrunch (Mar 2021): Instagram Live "isn't really built for things like bidding or handling payments after a sale occurs." LaFontaine: existing live auctions were "all hacked together, people were bidding in the comments" (NEA). A 2026 analysis credits Whatnot with integrating "payments, logistics, and moderation into the live experience." The first stream's result (more in 2.5 hours than a year on eBay) is a direct comparison of formats with the same seller and products.

**Against.** Competitors with the same mechanics existed (Loupe from Oct 2020), so integrated buying alone does not explain why Whatnot won. The first-stream comparison is one founder-reported anecdote.

**Verdict: supported.** The decisive reason in 2020; a necessary condition afterwards.

## 4. The communities

### Funko Pops
- **Before Whatnot:** collectors traded Funko on marketplaces (LaFontaine sold them on eBay). Live auctions of Disney pins on Instagram Live are documented; Funko live selling before Whatnot is plausible but I found no dated source showing it directly.
- **Joined Whatnot:** from the start (Dec 2019 marketplace; first live stream July 2020). Funko-only until October 2020.
- **How big:** third behind sports cards and Pokémon by September 2021.
- **Captured or new:** captured. Funko sellers heard about the first stream and wanted in; buyers came from the existing collector base.

### Sports cards
- **Before Whatnot:** the clearest pre-existing live behaviour. Breaks on YouTube, Twitter, Breakers TV and Instagram since well before 2020; surge from mid-March 2020 (SI; LA Times). Group-break slots sold by team, cards sorted and mailed afterwards.
- **Joined Whatnot:** January 2021, about ten months after the breaking surge began and after rival Loupe launched.
- **How big:** 80x growth by May 2021 and largest category; "millions of dollars" per month; still the biggest driver in September 2021.
- **Captured or new:** captured. Whatnot became a better venue for an existing break economy (payment and slots handled in-app). Whether break buyers were new to the hobby is not established, though SI noted breaks were already pulling in younger buyers before the pandemic.

### Pokémon
- **Before Whatnot:** strong existing demand (eBay +574% in 2020; Target pulled cards in May 2021). I found no dated source on Pokémon-specific live selling before Whatnot.
- **Joined Whatnot:** late 2020, alongside the switch to live auctions (TechCrunch Dec 2020 headline pairs them).
- **How big:** second-largest driver by September 2021.
- **Captured or new:** captured demand that was already surging. LaFontaine himself grew up selling Pokémon cards and ran an eBay store in fourth grade (Fortune), so the founders were insiders, not outsiders inventing a market.

## 5. What this means for eBay Live (hypotheses only)

1. **H1 (top-down vs bottom-up):** Whatnot's top-down marketing came *after* a bottom-up base was working. eBay Live is running the top-down push first. In categories where live buying already exists (cards, Pokémon), the push may work by redirecting existing behaviour; in categories without it (cameras), there is no Whatnot precedent showing ads alone create the habit.
2. **H2 (reaching people in feeds):** Whatnot's 2022 ad money went 99% to Instagram, the platform where live collectible auctions began. This supports Carly's point: go to the feeds where the community already is, rather than waiting for people to search for live shopping.
3. **H3 (growing the category):** Whatnot did not grow the category from zero; it rode a boom (eBay's own data shows trading cards +142% in 2020). Category-wide growth may depend more on whether the underlying hobby is growing than on the live product. eBay's own "State of Trading Cards" data can test which communities are growing now.
4. **H4 (60-second measure):** Whatnot's survey shows existing buyers watch mainly "to get a product I want" (66%) and for deals (53%), ahead of entertainment (46%). If viewers come for a specific product, watch time may matter less than whether the wanted item is on the stream. A 60+ second view may not predict buying unless the right inventory is live.
5. **H5 (where eBay can win):** the gap Whatnot fixed in 2020 was mechanics (bidding, payment, shipping in-stream). eBay already has those, plus trust and authentication. That suggests eBay's advantage is strongest where communities still run live sales on poorly built tools, and weakest where Whatnot and Fanatics Live already own the break economy.

## 7. Gaps

1. **Did buyers move over, or were they new to collecting?** No source measures this for 2020 to 2022. Possible public routes: Reddit and forum threads from 2020 to 2021 (r/baseballcards, r/PokemonTCG, Blowout Forums) where people describe arriving on Whatnot; early seller interviews.
2. **Did named sellers bring audiences?** Not found. Phase 2 should trace specific breakers and Instagram auctioneers (YouTube channel histories, Instagram bios linking to Whatnot, Sports Collectors Daily coverage; its May 2021 article on Whatnot breaks returned an error here and is worth retrying).
3. **Why Whatnot beat Loupe and other apps with the same mechanics.** Not established. Candidates: category breadth, funding, seller economics (8% take). Needs comparative reporting.
4. **Pokémon live selling before Whatnot.** Demand is documented; the live-selling behaviour is not. YouTube pack-opening history would answer it.
5. **Category split of GMV.** Only rank order (cards > Pokémon > Funko) is public for 2021.
6. **Why people first try live shopping.** Whatnot's survey covers current buyers only. Independent research found here measures non-interest (43%) but not the trigger for a first purchase. Phase 7 territory.
7. **The 2024 "$3B" figure** is only seen via a secondary site; the original Whatnot post should be read directly.
